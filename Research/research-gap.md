# Research Gap

## What is already established

The reviewed literature supports five boundaries on the project’s novelty claim:

1. Interaction latency can impair target selection, pointing, aiming, and tracking.
2. Latency with moving targets has already been investigated.
3. Motion characteristics can moderate latency costs in desktop game-like acquisition.
4. Predictability can influence adaptation to delayed visual feedback.
5. Controlled latency has already been studied in immersive VR target selection and in FPS aiming.

Accordingly, the project does not claim that latency with moving targets, FPS latency, immersive-VR selection latency, or latency-by-predictability relationships are unexplored.

## Closest prior work

Claypool, Cockburn, and Gutwin (2020) is the closest verified precedent in causal structure. It crossed controlled input delay with target jink angle and jink interval and found significant interactions for selection time and click accuracy in a desktop game-like task.

Pavlovych and Gutwin (2012) is the strongest full-primary-source precedent for latency during complex moving-target acquisition and tracking. Its 20/50/110/170 ms conditions were network round-trip latency values, not added local cursor latency. It examined target speed, path complexity, acquisition attempts, and longitudinal and transverse error, but did not use an immersive HMD or an isolated predictability factor.

Rohde, van Dam, and Ernst (2014) provides the strongest construct-level precedent: predictability was manipulated while the underlying reference trajectory was held constant, and predictability affected adaptation to delayed visual feedback. Its task was nonimmersive continuous manual tracking rather than rapid first-shot acquisition.

Wang, Sabiston, and Welsh (2026) is the closest modern immersive-interaction precedent, but it is a preprint and used static targets. Wei et al. (2026) establishes peer-reviewed VR target-selection work under latency, while the verified abstract record for Ivkovic et al. (2015) establishes controlled FPS targeting and tracking under local latency. Neither authoritative record supports a claim that its study independently manipulated target-motion predictability.

## Methodological limitation in prior predictability manipulations

The most relevant moving-target studies do not isolate predictability from target kinematics.

- In Claypool et al. (2020), jink angle and jink interval altered how predictable the target was, but also affected its movement dynamics and effective speed.
- In Pavlovych and Gutwin (2012), greater path complexity was related to lower predictability, but it also changed instantaneous-velocity characteristics despite efforts to match average amplitude and velocity.

These studies therefore establish latency interactions with motion dynamics or complexity, not an orthogonal interaction with predictability alone. Rohde et al. (2014) manipulated predictability more cleanly, but in a different, noncompetitive tracking-and-adaptation paradigm.

## Unresolved question

Within the supplied evidence, it remains unclear whether latency-related effects observed in desktop moving-target acquisition and delayed visuomotor tracking generalize to embodied competitive aiming in an immersive HMD when target-motion predictability is manipulated independently of target kinematics.

It is also unresolved whether latency and predictability relate differently to overall acquisition time, the initial shot decision, and the subsequent feedback-driven adjustments visible in continuous aim trajectories. Prior studies generally emphasize selection time, endpoint accuracy, success rate, or aggregate tracking error rather than jointly examining acquisition time, first-shot behavior, and online corrective aiming in this setting.

## Defensible contribution

The defensible contribution is a controlled conjunction and behavioral decomposition: examining system-induced controller-to-weapon latency together with independently manipulated target-motion predictability during rapid immersive HMD target acquisition, while distinguishing acquisition time, first-shot accuracy, and online corrective aiming behavior.

This contribution should be framed as testing whether findings from established adjacent paradigms generalize to a particular immersive competitive context. It should not be described as the first-ever study of latency and predictability, moving-target latency, FPS latency, or VR target-selection latency.
